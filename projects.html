Mechanical Design - MEng Thesis

Engineering Design of Actuated Sensor Alignment Systems for Subsea Applications

Engineering design of an actuated sensor alignment system for a gravity-moored subsea sensor platform.

+

Overview

This project investigates the engineering design of an actuated sensor alignment system for a gravity-moored subsea platform. The system is intended to compensate for sensor misalignment caused by tidal forces and maintain the required sensor orientation at depths of up to 100 m.

Project objectives

  • Develop an engineering design specification for the alignment system.
  • Investigate suitable actuator configurations.
  • Evaluate appropriate materials and mechanical components.
  • Analyse the structural behaviour of the proposed design.
  • Use engineering analysis, including FEA and CFD where appropriate.

Technical focus

The project combines mechanical design, actuator selection, structural analysis and subsea engineering considerations. Particular attention is being given to the effects of hydrostatic pressure, tidal loading and actuator requirements.

Computational Fluid Dynamics

Computational Fluid Dynamics Projects

CFD studies investigating internal pipe flow, high-speed train aerodynamics and fluid forces using STAR-CCM+. Completed as assessment in university course lead by Professor David Ingram.

+

1. CFD Simulation of Flow Through an Orifice Plate

Designed and simulated an orifice plate flow meter in a 280 mm diameter pipe using STAR-CCM+, with the aim of predicting the pressure differential across the plate under high-pressure nitrogen flow conditions. The design was developed with reference to BS EN ISO 5167, considering plate geometry, diameter ratio and pressure-tapping locations.

Developed a two-dimensional axisymmetric model using the SST k-ω turbulence model. Investigated mesh refinement, near-wall prism layers, turbulence modelling and the inlet length required to establish fully developed flow. Analysed pressure and velocity fields to identify flow separation, recirculation regions and downstream pressure recovery.

Compared the simulated pressure differential against the theoretical prediction from the ISO 5167 correlations. The initial comparison revealed a significant discrepancy, highlighting the importance of mesh quality, boundary conditions and further validation before CFD results can be used for design.

Key technical areas

  • STAR-CCM+ and axisymmetric CFD modelling
  • SST k-ω turbulence modelling
  • Mesh refinement and near-wall treatment
  • ISO 5167 orifice meter design and pressure-drop calculations
  • Critical evaluation of numerical results against theory

2. CFD Simulation of a High-Speed Shinkansen in the Channel Tunnel

Investigated the aerodynamic effects of operating an E5/H5-class Shinkansen at 313 km/h in the Channel Tunnel, significantly above the existing 160 km/h speed limit considered in the study. Using STAR-CCM+, modelled the interaction between the train and tunnel to assess pressure changes, aerodynamic forces and the flow structures generated around the train.

Developed a three-dimensional model of a four-carriage train within the tunnel, using a simplified geometry to balance physical detail and computational cost. Applied a compressible ideal-gas model and SST k-ω turbulence modelling, with local mesh refinement around the train nose, tail and carriage gaps. Conducted a grid-convergence study using the train drag coefficient and compared steady and unsteady solutions.

The simulations identified accelerated flow between the train and tunnel walls, substantial wake regions and pressure variations along the train. The grid study showed a consistent convergence trend, but numerical uncertainty remained because computational resources limited further refinement. This provided an opportunity to assess the limitations of the model when interpreting engineering loads.

Key technical areas

  • Three-dimensional external and internal flow modelling
  • Compressible flow and ideal-gas modelling
  • Mesh refinement and grid-convergence analysis
  • Boundary-layer and prism-layer meshing
  • Aerodynamic drag, lift and tunnel-wall loading
  • Evaluation of numerical uncertainty and computational constraints

Advanced Composite Materials

Advanced Composite Materials: Testing and Design

Experimental characterisation of carbon fibre composites and finite element analysis of composite laminate design using Abaqus and eLamX2.

+

1. Experimental Characterisation of Carbon Fibre Composites

Manufactured and experimentally characterised carbon fibre–epoxy composite laminates using T700G carbon fibres and VTC401 epoxy prepreg. Conducted tensile, compression and flexural testing on unidirectional and multidirectional laminates to investigate the influence of fibre orientation on mechanical properties and failure behaviour.

Determined elastic moduli, tensile and compressive strengths, and flexural properties from experimental data. Compared measured results against micromechanics predictions and manufacturer datasheets to assess the accuracy of theoretical models and material specifications.

Experimental results demonstrated the strong anisotropy of composite materials. The quasi-isotropic laminate exhibited 50.7% greater flexural modulus and 24.8% greater flexural strength than the cross-ply laminate. Failure analysis identified fibre fracture, matrix cracking, delamination and fibre microbuckling, depending on loading direction and laminate configuration.

Key technical areas

  • Composite laminate manufacture and vacuum-bag curing
  • Tensile, compression and three-point flexural testing
  • Carbon fibre–epoxy material characterisation
  • Micromechanics and comparison with manufacturer data
  • Stress–strain analysis and composite failure mechanisms
  • Effect of fibre orientation and laminate stacking sequence

2. Composite Laminate Analysis and Design Optimisation

Applied classical composite laminate plate theory to analyse the stiffness, strains and ply-level stresses of a composite plate under uniaxial loading. Derived laminate ABD matrices and calculated ply-level responses, validating analytical results against eLamX2.

Conducted finite element analysis in Abaqus on a 16-ply composite plate containing a hole, subjected to tensile and shear-bearing loads. Used the Tsai–Wu failure criterion to evaluate critical plies, estimate safety factors and identify stress concentrations around the hole.

Evaluated five alternative stacking sequences to improve performance under both loading conditions. The selected layup increased the predicted safety factor from 4.43 to 6.84 in tension and from 3.81 to 5.51 in shear. The results demonstrated how balancing 0°, ±45° and 90° plies can improve load distribution and reduce critical failure indices.

Critically assessed the limitations of the finite element model, including idealised ply bonding, manufacturing defects, mesh sensitivity and the absence of progressive damage and delamination modelling.

Key technical areas

  • Classical laminate plate theory and ABD matrices
  • Ply-level stress and strain analysis
  • eLamX2 analytical calculations and validation
  • Abaqus finite element analysis
  • Tsai–Wu failure criterion and safety-factor assessment
  • Stacking-sequence optimisation under tension and shear
  • Evaluation of FEA assumptions and modelling limitations

Digital Manufacturing

Digital Manufacturing of Motorsport Components

Investigation into reverse engineering, generative design, additive manufacturing and dimensional verification of a motorsport component.

+

Overview

Investigated the feasibility of using digital manufacturing technologies to replace existing aluminium brackets on a 1997 Lola T97/20 Indy Lights car with an optimised alternative. The assignment explored the integration of reverse engineering, generative design, additive manufacturing and dimensional verification within the component development process.

1. Reverse Engineering

Investigated optical reverse engineering techniques using a Faro QuantumE FaroArm and an Einstar handheld 3D scanner to digitally recreate an existing aluminium bracket. Evaluated scanning resolution, accuracy, accessibility and equipment costs, considering the suitability of different technologies for the component geometry.

Generated a point cloud containing approximately 1.9 million data points and converted the scanned geometry into an STL mesh for CAD reconstruction. Critically evaluated the limitations of optical scanning, including line-of-sight restrictions, measurement uncertainty and the computational requirements of processing high-resolution scan data.

Compared optical scanning against conventional manual measurement techniques. Concluded that manual metrology would be more cost-effective for the relatively simple bracket geometry, with optical scanning better suited to complex or organic components.

2. Generative Design and Structural Optimisation

Recreated the original aluminium 6061 bracket in Autodesk Fusion and conducted finite element analysis under a 3 kN loading condition applied at 30 degrees to the horizontal. The initial design produced a minimum safety factor of 1.436, below the target safety factor of 4.

Undertook manual structural optimisation by modifying the base and side-panel thicknesses and removing material from low-stress regions. Achieved a 15% reduction in mass, from 41.1 g to 34.9 g, while maintaining a safety factor of 4.171.

Subsequently investigated generative design using Autodesk Fusion, defining preserved and obstacle geometries alongside structural loading and manufacturing constraints. The resulting design achieved a mass of 28 g, representing a 31.9% reduction compared with the original bracket. However, independent FEA validation identified safety factors of 1.044 and 2.109 for the generated designs, demonstrating the importance of validating computationally generated geometries against engineering requirements.

3. Additive Manufacturing

Developed additive manufacturing strategies for both fused filament fabrication (FFF) and laser powder bed fusion (LPBF). Using PrusaSlicer, investigated alternative build orientations for polymer printing, evaluating support material requirements, build time, dimensional accuracy and surface quality.

Extended the investigation to a NACA 4418 turbine blade, assessing the effect of build orientation on aerodynamic surface quality, support requirements and manufacturing efficiency.

Prepared LPBF builds for the standard and generatively designed brackets using Autodesk Netfabb. Evaluated build orientation, support structures, machining allowances and the influence of layer orientation on mechanical performance. The generative bracket required 11.27 cm³ of material and an estimated build time of 3 hours 56 minutes, compared with 16.36 cm³ and 5 hours 42 minutes for the standard bracket.

Critically assessed the suitability of additive manufacturing against conventional processes, considering production volume, material waste, post-processing requirements and dimensional limitations.

4. Dimensional Metrology and Tolerance Analysis

Investigated dimensional verification of manufactured components using a Faro QuantumE FaroArm and Faro Vantage Laser Tracker. Developed a measurement strategy based on the functional requirements of the bracket, identifying critical geometrical features through FEA results.

Evaluated measurement uncertainty, datum selection and the application of geometrical dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T). Used the simulated deformation and stress distribution to establish appropriate tolerances for critical features, including side-panel hole positions and internal spacing.

Produced an engineering drawing with functional tolerances in accordance with ISO 1101:2017. Considered the relationship between manufacturing variation, measurement uncertainty and structural performance when determining appropriate acceptance criteria.

Key technical areas

  • Optical 3D scanning and reverse engineering
  • CAD modelling and generative design using Autodesk Fusion
  • Finite element analysis and structural optimisation
  • Fused filament fabrication (FFF) and PrusaSlicer
  • Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and Autodesk Netfabb
  • Design for additive manufacturing
  • Dimensional metrology and measurement uncertainty
  • Geometrical dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T)

Mechanical Design | Hyperloop

Fail-Safe Friction Braking System – HYPED

Design, manufacture and testing of an electrically and pneumatically fail-safe braking system for Edinburgh Hyperloop's Pod Ness prototype.

+

Overview

As Friction Brakes Project Manager within Edinburgh Hyperloop's Dynamic Subteam, I led the design and development of a mechanical braking system for the 2023 Pod Ness prototype. The system provided independent emergency braking alongside the linear induction motor, automatically engaging following electrical or pneumatic failure.

Mechanical Design and Analysis

Developed a spring-applied, pneumatically released braking mechanism using SolidWorks CAD. Carried out engineering calculations and component selection to meet braking performance, packaging and safety requirements. Used finite element analysis (FEA) to assess structural integrity and optimise component geometry, achieving the required minimum safety factor of 2.

Manufacture, Integration and Testing

Led the manufacture and assembly of the brake modules, working with University workshop technicians to develop manufacturing drawings and improve manufacturability. Coordinated with Electronics and Software subteams to integrate the system and produce the Testing and Safety Documentation.

Conducted pneumatic and mechanical testing to validate fail-safe operation, actuator performance, brake clearance and friction characteristics. Power-loss testing demonstrated that the pneumatic circuit vented completely in under one second, confirming its electrically fail-safe operation.

Key Skills

  • SolidWorks CAD and engineering drawings
  • Finite Element Analysis (FEA)
  • Mechanical and pneumatic system design
  • Engineering calculations and component selection
  • Manufacture, assembly and physical testing
  • Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA)
  • Multidisciplinary project management

Renewable Energy | Interdisciplinary Design

Run-of-River Hydropower Scheme – Glen Douchary

Interdisciplinary design of an 800 kW run-of-river hydropower scheme in North West Scotland.

+

Overview

As part of an interdisciplinary group design project at the University of Edinburgh, worked within a team of mechanical, civil and electrical engineers to develop a preliminary design for a run-of-river hydropower scheme at Glen Douchary, near Ullapool in North West Scotland.

The proposed scheme incorporated a weir, penstock, turbine, generator and grid connection, with consideration given to engineering feasibility, environmental impact and economic viability. The final design proposed an 800 kW installation with an estimated annual energy generation of 2.78 GWh.

My Contribution

Responsible for researching the project background and existing hydropower precedents, assessing the catchment area and contributing to the selection of the proposed scheme location. Evaluated geographical, environmental and infrastructure considerations to inform the wider engineering design.

Also led the compilation and formatting of the final group report using LaTeX, integrating contributions from the different engineering disciplines into a single technical document.

Engineering Evaluation

Produced an individual evaluation of the proposed scheme, assessing its ability to meet the original design requirements. Critically examined its economic feasibility, environmental impact and practical challenges, identifying limitations in the financial assumptions and the potential impact of construction within a protected landscape.

Consulted Alex Reading, CEO of Green Highland Renewables and Chairman of the British Hydropower Association, to obtain industry feedback on the scheme's feasibility. Also evaluated the effectiveness of group coordination and the challenges of delivering an interdisciplinary engineering project.

Final Scheme Design

  • 800 kW installed generating capacity
  • 2.78 GWh estimated annual energy generation
  • 85.5 m net hydraulic head
  • 2.3 km penstock pipeline
  • Francis Lo turbine selection
  • Approximately £2.33 million capital cost
  • Approximately 9.75-year payback period

Key Technical Areas

  • Renewable energy and hydropower engineering
  • Site selection and catchment area assessment
  • Engineering feasibility and design evaluation
  • Environmental and economic considerations
  • Interdisciplinary engineering collaboration
  • Technical report writing and LaTeX documentation
  • Project coordination and critical evaluation
